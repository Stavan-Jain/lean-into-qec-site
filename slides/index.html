Right, so this tutorial is really about Lean, a special type of programming language called a proof assistant. Lean lets you write mathematical definitions — [IsEven] read it aloud: n is even when there exists a k with n = k + k. Definitions can be programs — [fib] here's Fibonacci, and Lean runs it like any other code. Then you write theorem statements about your own definitions — [fib_six_even] fib 6 is 8, and 8 is 4 + 4, so it's even. That's a claim, not a computation: it needs a proof. And you write proofs in Lean's syntax — [and_swap] here's a first theorem with its proof; don't worry about the syntax yet, we'll do this live in a few minutes. Statements can be about the library's definitions — [irrational_sqrt_two] mathlib is a community-driven library of formalized mathematics; this is its statement that √2 is irrational. They can be about our own field — [X_on_ket0] the bit flip: X applied to |0⟩ gives |1⟩, as QECLean states it — after lunch every symbol in that line will be a definition you have read. And they scale all the way to research — [flt] the statement of Fermat's Last Theorem, word for word as mathlib states it. Nobody has proved it in Lean: the cubes case is done there today, and proving the full statement is an active multi-year formalization project led by Kevin Buzzard. In an hour you'll be proving statements like the ones at the start.

Importantly, when you write a proof of a theorem in Lean, there's a small part of the Lean compiler — the kernel — whose only job is to check whether the proof truly proves the theorem in question. [and_swap] Take our proof from a minute ago — [kernel + ✓] the kernel checks it: accepted. [fermat] So if I put in my ingenious proof of Fermat's Last Theorem — [kernel + ✗] Lean will complain to me. It doesn't matter who you are or how confident you feel; the kernel is not impressed.

Another cool feature of Lean is that it is interactive: you can step through the proof and see the remaining proof obligations change. [caret at by] When my cursor is here, Lean shows me what there is to prove: given propositions p and q, that p ∧ q implies q ∧ p. [after intro h] The intro step moves the hypothesis into my context — I now have h, a proof of p ∧ q, and the goal has changed. [after constructor] constructor splits the conjunction — I now owe two things: a proof of q, and a proof of p. [before exact h.right] The little dot focuses the first goal — everything else disappears until it's done — and exact h.right will discharge it. [before exact h.left] The second dot focuses what remains: one obligation, a proof of p. [end] And at the end — no goals. The proof is complete. A Brief History of Lean

2013: Lean is born at Microsoft Research — Leonardo de Moura, creator of the Z3 SMT solver, starts it as a research project to bridge the gap between automated and interactive theorem proving. Even its earliest users were formalizing mathematics — homotopy type theory, category theory, abstract algebra — mostly type theorists and logicians. 2017: the working mathematicians arrive in force — Kevin Buzzard and others start using it, and mathlib forms: an open-source, community-driven effort to formalize mathematics. 2019 to 2022: proof that Lean can handle research-level mathematics — perfectoid spaces are formalized in 2019, and Peter Scholze poses the Liquid Tensor Experiment in December 2020; the key theorem is verified within six months and the full challenge completed in July 2022. Up to this point, writing formal proofs was painstaking — roughly ten times the effort of the handwritten proof. Then AI arrived. People started building tools that produce Lean proofs rather than natural-language proofs, precisely so the output can be trusted. 2024: DeepMind's AlphaProof scores 28 out of 42 at the IMO — silver-medal standard, one point below gold, the first AI to reach a medal tier. 2025: Harmonic's Aristotle reaches gold-medal performance — five of six problems, and unlike the natural-language golds that year, every proof formally verified in Lean.

Collaboration at Scale

stated formalized — kernel-checked
In November 2023, Terence Tao and three coauthors proved a well-known conjecture in additive combinatorics, the Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture. [graph] Within days of the paper going up, Tao posted a Lean blueprint of the proof — a human-readable outline of the argument where every lemma links to its formal counterpart, with a dependency graph. Hollow nodes are stated; green nodes are formalized. Initially, nothing was proved. [first wave] People from the community started picking off pieces, and each time someone finished one, the blueprint updated and linked to the formalization. [all green] About three weeks later [verify], the whole proof was formalized — with contributions from roughly two dozen people [verify], most of whom Tao had never met. That's the point: you can build on a stranger's part of the proof because the Lean kernel has already checked it. Trust doesn't have to come from knowing your collaborators. [no build — spoken over the finished graph] More recently, Kevin Buzzard started a project to formalize Fermat's Last Theorem — the statement you read this morning; it was two lines. That project has over fifty contributors [verify] and is expected to take years — nobody thinks it's close to done. But Lean is what lets an effort like that scale at all. That is the general case. Now the case for QEC in particular.

Why Formalize QEC?

1Verified discovery of new codes
2Trust across the stack
So why do this for QEC in particular? Two reasons. [1] AI writes; the kernel checks. The folklore was that a formal proof costs an order of magnitude more than the paper proof. That is changing: write the statement, hand lemma-scale goals to an agent, and it closes many of them. You still own the definitions and the statements — the part that needs your expertise, not Lean expertise. And the relationship flips: the results on the news slide this morning were produced by AI and verified by Lean. For QEC specifically, a model can search a combinatorial space of codes and gadgets; the checker is what turns "the model proposed a code" into "a code with a proved distance." [2] Trust across the stack. QEC sits between physics, coding theory, decoders, compilers and hardware, and the people downstream cannot check a distance proof by hand. A kernel-checked theorem is a certificate they can consume without reading the proof — they read the statement, and checking a statement is much cheaper than checking a proof. The rest of the stack already works this way: verified quantum compilers exist, in Coq. QEC theory is the layer that has been missing.

Seven nouns and one predicate carry all of stabilizer error correction, and each is a definition in QECLean. For each: the mathematics first, one piece at a time, then the Lean, one field at a time. [Pauli operator] A single-qubit Pauli — [math] one of I, X, Y, Z. [Lean] An inductive type — [constructors] with four constructors. That is the whole definition. [n-qubit operator] [math] A tensor product of n of them, [math] each an I, X, Y or Z — [math] for instance X on the first qubit and Z on the second. [Lean] A definition — [body] a function from qubit index to Pauli. [example] And that operator written down as one: qubit zero gets X, qubit one gets Z. [notation] Or with the library's notation, which means exactly the same thing: P for a bare Pauli string, then the letters, one per qubit. The σ[ZZZIZII] you'll see in the editor is this notation with a phase in front — a group element, the next rung. [group element] [math] Multiply by a phase, [math] one of four, [math] and you have an element of the Pauli group. [Lean] A structure — [phasePower] the phase, as a number mod 4, [operators] and the string. [stabilizer group] [math] A subgroup of the Pauli group, [math] abelian, [math] not containing minus the identity. [Lean] A structure — [toSubgroup] the subgroup, [is_abelian] the abelian condition — the theorem you just read — [no_neg_identity] and the exclusion of −I. [stabilizer code] Now the definition you know. [math] n − k generators, [math] independent and pairwise commuting, [math] generating a group that does not contain −I. That's it — the code is its stabilizer group. [Lean] The structure you will watch me fill in for Steane, one field at a time — [hk] k at most n, [generatorsList] the generator list, [length] with n − k entries, [phaseZero] each with phase one, [independent] independent, [commute] pairwise commuting, [no −I] and no minus identity in the group they generate. The field names are the checklist. One honest footnote for the demo: the library's structure also asks for a chosen logical basis — an X̄ and Z̄ per logical qubit — because the transversal-gate and concatenation theorems need one. That is packaging, not definition, and I've prepared it. [logical operators] [math] The centralizer of S: everything that commutes with every stabilizer. [math] A logical operator is an element of the centralizer — it preserves the code space. [math] It is nontrivial when it is not itself a stabilizer, even up to a phase. [Lean] The centralizer, which is mathlib's centralizer of the underlying subgroup. [IsNontrivial] And the predicate you will see inside the distance definition: in the centralizer — [phase] and no stabilizer has the same Pauli string, which is "not in S up to phase". Every name in the distance clause is now defined. [distance] Now distance. [math] The minimum weight of a nontrivial logical operator — weight meaning the number of qubits it touches, and nontrivial meaning it commutes with S but is not itself a stabilizer up to phase. [Lean] Now the specification — the part I want you to be able to read and judge. [d ≥ 1] Distance is at least one. [∀] Every nontrivial logical operator of positive weight has weight at least d — "minimum" as a lower bound. And notice the extra condition, zero less than the weight of g: it excludes weight-zero elements. Is it needed, given how "nontrivial" is defined on the left? That is exactly the question a referee should ask — a question about error correction, not about Lean. [∃] And some nontrivial logical operator has weight exactly d — "minimum" as attained. Every noun in the textbook is a def; every condition is a field or a clause. That is the whole trick, and checking that the clauses say what the left-hand side says is your expertise, not Lean expertise. [[[n, k, d]]] And the package — this one has no textbook sentence, because it is packaging. [Lean] A structure that extends StabilizerCode — every field of the code, [hasDistance] plus one more: a proof that the code has distance d. All three parameters in the type. The Steane file ends by building one of these, so distance three is not a claim in a comment but a field the kernel has checked.

The instance: the Steane code, built the way the textbook builds it. [H] Start classical. This is the parity-check matrix of the [7,4,3] Hamming code — three rows, seven columns, and the columns are the seven nonzero vectors of length three; that is what gives the classical code distance three. [Z rows] Now read each row as a Pauli string: a 1 becomes Z, a 0 becomes I. Three Z-checks. [X rows] Do it again with X. Three X-checks. That is the CSS construction, and it works here because every pair of rows of H overlaps in an even number of places — so every Z-check commutes with every X-check. In a minute the kernel will check that rather than take my word for it. [parameters] Six generators on seven qubits leave one logical qubit — and distance three, which for now is a claim.
